(Q.No. 1)

1.What is the offence of voluntarily causing hurt by means of poison under the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023, and what is the punishment provided for it?[5]
Ans. The offence is aimed at administering a noxious thing as the means to another crime. Whoever administers to, or causes to be taken by, any person poison, or any stupefying, intoxicating or unwholesome drug, with intent to cause hurt, or to commit or facilitate an offence, or knowing it likely that hurt will be caused, is punishable with ten years and fine (Section 123).

(Q.No. 2)

2.What is the offence of unlawful compulsory labour under the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023?[5]
Ans. Compelling a person to labour against his will is itself the offence; no hurt or confinement need be shown. Whoever unlawfully compels any person to labour against the will of that person is punishable with imprisonment of either description up to one year, or with fine, or with both (Section 146). The provision gives effect to the prohibition of forced labour in Article 23 of the Constitution.

(Q.No. 3)

3.Explain the offences of voluntarily causing hurt for the purpose of extorting property, of extorting a confession, and of deterring a public servant from the discharge of his duty, under the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023. State the punishment provided in each case.[10]
Ans. All three punish hurt used as a means to obtain something else, each graded by whether the hurt is simple or grievous.
Hurt to extort from the sufferer any property or valuable security, or to constrain him to do anything illegal or which may facilitate an offence, draws ten years and fine; grievous hurt so caused draws life, or ten years, and fine (Section 119).
Hurt to extort a confession, or information leading to detection of an offence or misconduct, or to constrain the sufferer to restore property or satisfy a claim, draws seven years and fine; grievous hurt for that purpose draws ten years and fine (Section 120).
Hurt to a public servant in the discharge of his duty, or to deter him or any other from discharging it, draws five years, or fine, or both; grievous hurt so caused draws one to ten years and fine (Section 121).

(Q.No. 4)

4.Explain the offence of kidnapping or abducting a person in order that such person may be murdered, and the offence of kidnapping or abducting for ransom, under the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023. Support your answer giving illustrations.[10]
Ans. Kidnapping or abducting any person in order that he may be murdered, or may be so disposed of as to be put in danger of being murdered, is punishable with life or rigorous imprisonment up to ten years, and fine (Section 140(1)). Illustration — A kidnaps Z intending to sacrifice him to an idol; A is liable though Z is rescued unharmed.
Kidnapping or abducting a person and keeping him in detention, and threatening to cause death or hurt, or by conduct giving rise to a reasonable apprehension of death or hurt, in order to compel the Government or any other person to do or abstain from any act or to pay a ransom, is punishable with death, or life, and fine (Section 140(2)). Illustration — A abducts a child and demands money from the father; A is liable, and it is immaterial that the demand is never met.

(Q.No. 5)

5.“Culpable homicide is not murder if the offender, whilst deprived of the power of self-control by grave and sudden provocation, causes the death of the person who gave the provocation or causes the death of any other person by mistake or accident.”
Explain the above exception to section 300 of the Indian Penal Code, 1860, and state the provisos to which it is subject. Discuss with the help of decisions of the Supreme Court.[10]
Ans. The exception reduces murder to culpable homicide not amounting to murder where the offender, deprived of the self-control by grave and sudden provocation, causes the death of the person who gave it, or of another by mistake or accident. The loss of self-control must be grave and sudden, the killing following before passion cools.
Three provisos qualify it: the provocation must not have been sought or voluntarily provoked as an excuse for killing; nor given by anything done in obedience to the law, or by a public servant lawfully exercising his powers; nor by anything done in lawful exercise of the right of private defence. Whether provocation was grave and sudden is a question of fact.
In K.M. Nanavati v. State of Maharashtra the test was held to be that of a reasonable person of the accused’s class, the exception failing where time had passed for passion to cool.

(Q.No. 6)

6.Explain the offences under sections 336, 337 and 338 of the Indian Penal Code, 1860 relating to acts done so rashly or negligently as to endanger human life or the personal safety of others. Point out the difference between them.[10]
Ans. The three rest on the same act — doing anything so rashly or negligently as to endanger human life or the personal safety of others — forming an ascending scale graded by consequence.
Where the act merely endangers life or personal safety and no injury follows, three months, or fine up to two hundred and fifty rupees, or both (Section 336).
Where such an act causes hurt, six months, or fine up to five hundred rupees, or both (Section 337).
Where it causes grievous hurt, two years, or fine up to one thousand rupees, or both (Section 338).
The difference lies not in the act but in its result: 336 punishes the risk alone, 337 the hurt caused, and 338 grievous hurt. Rashness is acting with awareness of the risk but hoping it will not follow; negligence is omitting the care a prudent man would take.

(Q.No. 7)

7.“Whoever causes death by doing an act with the intention of causing death, or with the intention of causing such bodily injury as is likely to cause death, or with the knowledge that he is likely by such act to cause death, commits the offence of culpable homicide.”
Comment on the above statement and distinguish culpable homicide from murder under the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023. State the punishment provided for murder and for culpable homicide not amounting to murder. Discuss with the help of decisions of the Supreme Court.[20]
Ans. The statement reproduces the definition of culpable homicide (Section 100): causing death by an act done with the intention of causing death, or of causing bodily injury likely to cause death, or knowing it likely to cause death.
Murder is its aggravated form. Culpable homicide is murder where the act is done with the intention of causing death; with the intention of causing such bodily injury as the offender knows to be likely to cause the death of the person harmed; with the intention of causing bodily injury sufficient in the ordinary course of nature to cause death; or with the knowledge that the act is so imminently dangerous that it must in all probability cause death, committed without excuse (Section 101).
Every murder is culpable homicide, but not every culpable homicide is murder. The distinction is one of degree: where death is a likely result the offence is culpable homicide, where it is the most probable result it is murder. In State of A.P. v. Rayavarapu Punnayya the Supreme Court set out the three-fold enquiry — whether the accused caused death, whether that was culpable homicide, and whether it fell within any clause of murder; and Virsa Singh v. State of Punjab laid down the four requirements of the third clause, the sufficiency of the injury being an objective enquiry.
Murder draws death or imprisonment for life, and fine; and where a group of five or more acting in concert commits murder on the ground of race, caste, community, sex, place of birth, language or personal belief, each member draws death or life, and fine (Section 103). Culpable homicide not amounting to murder draws life, or five to ten years, and fine, where done with intention; and up to ten years and fine where done with knowledge only (Section 105).

(Q.No. 8)

8.“Whoever voluntarily causes hurt, if the hurt which he intends to cause or knows himself to be likely to cause is grievous hurt, and if the hurt which he causes is grievous hurt, is said voluntarily to cause grievous hurt.”
Comment on the above statement and explain the aggravated forms of this offence under the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023, including the punishment where the hurt causes permanent disability or a persistent vegetative state, and where grievous hurt is caused by a group of five or more persons acting in concert on the ground of race, caste or community.[20]
Ans. The statement is the definition of voluntarily causing grievous hurt. Two limbs must concur: the hurt intended or known to be likely must be grievous, and the hurt actually caused must be grievous; a grievous hurt caused while intending only simple hurt does not attract it (Section 117(1)).
What is grievous is exhaustively listed — emasculation; permanent privation of the sight of either eye; permanent privation of the hearing of either ear; privation of any member or joint; destruction or permanent impairing of the powers of any member or joint; permanent disfiguration of the head or face; fracture or dislocation of a bone or tooth; and any hurt which endangers life, or which causes the sufferer to be during twenty days in severe bodily pain or unable to follow his ordinary pursuits (Section 116). The simple offence carries imprisonment up to seven years and fine.
The Sanhita adds two aggravated forms. Where, in the course of committing the offence, the offender causes hurt which puts the sufferer in permanent disability or in a persistent vegetative state, the punishment is rigorous imprisonment not less than ten years, extending to imprisonment for life, which shall mean imprisonment for the remainder of that person’s natural life (Section 117(2)).
Where a group of five or more persons acting in concert causes grievous hurt on the ground of race, caste or community, sex, place of birth, language, personal belief or any other similar ground, each member of the group is guilty of causing grievous hurt and is punishable with imprisonment up to seven years and fine (Section 117(4)). Liability is fixed on every member individually, so acting as part of the concerted group suffices; the provision answers the mischief of targeted mob violence.

(Q.No. 9)

9.“Whoever wrongfully restrains any person in such a manner as to prevent that person from proceeding beyond certain circumscribing limits, is said wrongfully to confine that person.”
Comment on the above statement and explain the aggravated forms of wrongful confinement under the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023, with reference to the period of confinement, confinement in secret, and confinement for the purpose of extorting property or a confession. Support your answer giving illustrations.[20]
Ans. The statement defines wrongful confinement, which is a species of wrongful restraint. Restraint is obstructing a person so as to prevent him from proceeding in a direction in which he has a right to proceed; confinement is restraint of such a kind as to keep him within certain circumscribing limits (Sections 126(1) and 127(1)). The limits must circumscribe, so a known and available route of escape negatives confinement, though a route the person cannot reasonably use does not.
Wrongful restraint is punishable with simple imprisonment up to one month, or fine up to five thousand rupees, or both (Section 126(2)); simple wrongful confinement with imprisonment up to one year, or fine up to five thousand rupees, or both (Section 127(2)).
The aggravated forms turn on period, secrecy and purpose. Confinement for three days or more draws three years, or fine up to ten thousand rupees, or both (Section 127(3)); for ten days or more, five years and fine not less than ten thousand rupees (Section 127(4)). Confining a person knowing that a writ for his liberation has been duly issued draws a further two years (Section 127(5)). Confinement kept secret from any person interested in the confined person, or from any public servant, draws a further term (Section 127(6)). Confinement for the purpose of extorting a confession, or information leading to the detection of an offence or misconduct, or to constrain restoration of property or satisfaction of a claim, draws three years and fine (Section 127(7)).
Illustration — A keeps Z within a walled compound and bolts the only gate; the confinement is complete though Z never attempts to leave.

(Q.No. 10)

10.“Whoever voluntarily causes a woman with child to miscarry, shall, if such miscarriage be not caused in good faith for the purpose of saving the life of the woman, be punished.”
Comment on the above statement with reference to section 312 of the Indian Penal Code, 1860. Distinguish it from causing miscarriage without the consent of the woman and from death caused by an act done with intent to cause miscarriage. Whether a woman who causes herself to miscarry is within the section? Explain it.[20]
Ans. Voluntarily causing a woman with child to miscarry is an offence unless the miscarriage is caused in good faith for the purpose of saving the life of the woman. It carries imprisonment up to three years, or fine, or both; and where the woman is quick with child, up to seven years and fine (Section 312). Quickening is the stage at which the mother first perceives the movement of the foetus, and the heavier punishment answers the advanced stage of the pregnancy. The consent of the woman is immaterial to the offence; the saving of life in good faith is the only exception.
It is distinguished from causing miscarriage without the consent of the woman, punishable with imprisonment for life, or up to ten years, and fine, whether or not she is quick with child (Section 313). Consent is the dividing line, and its absence makes the offence far graver.
It is further distinguished from death caused by an act done with intent to cause miscarriage. Where the act causes the death of the woman the punishment is up to ten years and fine; and where it is done without her consent, imprisonment for life or up to ten years and fine. It is immaterial that the offender did not know the act was likely to cause death (Section 314).
As to the last question, the Explanation to section 312 is express: a woman who causes herself to miscarry is within the section. She is liable as a principal offender, a departure from the rule that the sufferer is not punished. It is against this that the Medical Termination of Pregnancy Act, 1971 operates, terminations made in accordance with it being protected.
